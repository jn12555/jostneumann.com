Tourism as a tool for development

September 2026 · approximately 5 minutes

For most of my working life I have dealt with one question: how rural areas can earn income from outside, first through investment in agriculture and later through tourism. Tourism brings visitors, and their spending, to places that other export sectors rarely reach. More arrivals do not by themselves mean development. Development happens when a larger share of that spending stays with local people and enterprises, and when communities have a say in how tourism grows.

Why tourism reaches where others do not

Tourism is consumed where it is produced. Visitors travel to the landscape, the village or the heritage site and pay there for accommodation, food, guiding, transport and crafts. For a rural area with few other exports, this is one of the rare ways to earn income from outside without people having to leave. The sector links to agriculture, fisheries and the creative industries.

It is also labour-intensive. According to ILO estimates, more than 230 million people, approximately 7.2 per cent of the global workforce, work in tourism industries, and the sector is "an important source of employment for women – including women entrepreneurs – and youth". The shares of women are highest in food and beverage services and high in accommodation. In the European Union, women held 57.6 per cent of jobs in selected tourism industries in 2023, against 36.4 per cent in the non-financial business economy, and 11.0 per cent of those employed were aged 15 to 24, against 9.0 per cent (Eurostat).

The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development reflects this. Three targets of the Sustainable Development Goals refer to tourism directly: SDG target 8.9 on policies for sustainable tourism that creates jobs and promotes local culture and products, SDG target 12.b on tools to monitor the impacts of such tourism, and SDG target 14.7 on the economic benefits that small island developing States and least developed countries draw from the sustainable use of marine resources, including tourism.

Conditions for development impact

  • Local linkages. Spending stays in the destination when hotels, restaurants and tour operators buy food, services and crafts locally, and when residents own and run enterprises themselves. Imported supplies and the transfer of profits by external owners are the main channels through which visitor spending leaves the host economy, as UNCTAD has set out. Older estimates put import-related leakage at 40–50 per cent of gross tourism earnings in small economies.
  • Skills and jobs. Training in hospitality, guiding, management and digital sales opens better-paid and year-round positions to residents, beyond seasonal entry-level work.
  • Shared infrastructure. Roads, water, energy and connectivity built for visitors should also serve the people who live there.
  • Participation. Communities need a voice in how, where and how much tourism develops, and a fair share of what it earns.
  • Heritage and nature. The cultural and natural assets that attract visitors need protection and investment, or the basis of the sector erodes.

Local evidence shows how much these conditions matter. A 2025 World Bank study of the areas around two national parks in Uganda found that non-local ownership and hiring are more common among tourism-related businesses, and that purchases from outside the local economy made up 42 per cent of retail spending near Bwindi Impenetrable National Park and 16 per cent near Queen Elizabeth National Park. In the study's model simulation, an additional USD 100 in revenue shared with communities raised local GDP by USD 245 around Queen Elizabeth and by USD 120 around the more isolated Bwindi, where more of the spending leaves the local economy.

Risks to manage

Tourism can also concentrate benefits in a few hands, raise land and housing prices, strain water supplies and turn cultural practices into staged products. Destinations that depend on one market or one season are exposed to crises, and many are directly exposed to climate change. These risks are an argument for planning tourism deliberately and for keeping it one part of a diversified local economy.

What public and development actors can do

Governments, development partners and foundations influence the outcome through decisions that are often taken far from the destination. Destination planning sets where and how much tourism develops. Access to finance decides whether local entrepreneurs or outside investors take up the opportunity: small rural enterprises are rarely bankable at first, and grants combined with technical assistance can bring them to that point. Investment in skills determines who gets the better jobs. Exchange between destinations that face the same problems lets practical experience travel from one place to the next.

In the grant portfolios for rural tourism I have managed, early-stage finance and basic business skills were more often the binding constraint than a lack of ideas or of visitor interest. I have seen good tourism products stall because nobody would finance the first season.

Measuring what development means

National series such as international tourist arrivals and international tourism receipts, which the World Bank publishes as open data, show the scale of the sector. They say little about development. More telling are the share of local ownership and employment, household income from tourism, the quality and seasonality of jobs, access to services and the condition of natural and cultural heritage. These indicators are harder to collect, especially for a district or a village, where official statistics are thin. They become useful when they are measured over time, compared across destinations and published as open data, so that public authorities, investors and communities can check progress against the same evidence.

What this means in practice

  • Map local supply first. Before promoting a destination, establish what hotels and operators buy and which of it local producers could supply with modest support, then agree local purchasing targets with the larger enterprises.
  • Count jobs by sex and age. Labour force surveys already allow this at national level, as the Eurostat figures show. A breakdown by district, where sample sizes allow, shows who takes the jobs in the destination.
  • Estimate local effects before spending. Local economy-wide models of the kind used in the World Bank study can compare revenue sharing, local sourcing and local ownership before a programme starts. Their results are estimates and should be checked against observed data later.
  • Finance the first season. Small grants or concessional loans combined with business training allow residents, and not only outside investors, to take up the opportunity.
  • Publish a few local indicators every year. Local ownership, local purchasing, employment by sex and age and the seasonality of jobs, published as open data, give authorities, investors and communities the same baseline.

My view is that tourism works as a tool for development where it is part of the local economy and is governed with the people it affects. The test I would apply is the change in their incomes and living conditions over time, and that change is what should be measured and published.
