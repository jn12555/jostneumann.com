Grants as a tool to de-risk investment

June 2026 · approximately 5 minutes

I came to grant-making from investment, after working on responsible investment in agriculture in Uganda, Zambia and Zimbabwe, and I still read a grant proposal partly as an investor would. Grant funding and investment are often managed by different teams under different rules. A grant designed with investment in mind can take on the early risks that keep private capital away from small enterprises and new markets, and prepare these enterprises for finance that has to be repaid.

The risks that keep capital away

Many enterprises with development potential, from rural guesthouses and tour operators to agricultural cooperatives, never reach an investor. The reasons are familiar: unproven business models, short financial records, little collateral, seasonal cash flows and, in many markets, currency risk. Small deal sizes add to the problem, because due diligence costs much the same for a small loan as for a large one. Together, these factors push the perceived risk above what commercial capital will accept, even where the underlying business is sound. In tourism, exposure to external shocks such as pandemics, natural hazards and sudden shifts in travel demand adds a further layer. The same constraints apply to impact investors, who invest with the intention to generate "positive, measurable social or environmental impact" alongside a financial return.

Grant instruments that reduce risk

  • Preparation grants finance feasibility studies, business plans and market assessments, so that projects reach investors ready for due diligence.
  • Technical assistance alongside a loan builds the bookkeeping, pricing, governance and reporting that lenders require, and lowers the risk of default.
  • First-loss and guarantee funding absorbs part of potential losses, so that lenders and investors can take positions they would otherwise decline.
  • Results-based payments reward verified social or environmental outcomes. Impact-linked finance applies this principle to enterprises directly: better verified results earn better financing terms, which improves the returns of those who deliver impact.
  • Market-building grants fund shared goods such as data, standards and certification, which lower costs for every investor in a sector. Open data on how enterprises in a sector perform helps investors price risks they would otherwise overestimate.

Development agencies are already shifting their mix. Convergence reports that they are moving concessional capital from grants to guarantees and other risk-sharing instruments: grants accounted for 41 per cent of it in 2022 and 10 per cent in 2024, while the share of guarantees rose from 11 to 27 per cent. A guarantee pays out only if losses occur, which can stretch a limited budget further. Grants remain the main tool where no repayment can be expected, as in preparation and technical assistance.

Evidence on outcomes is thinner, and much of it comes from implementers. Root Capital reports that in one impact-linked finance programme, USD 1 million in outcome payments enabled it to disburse USD 12 million in loans to 32 early-stage agricultural small and medium-sized enterprises, which paid USD 41 million to 9,300 smallholder farmers. These figures are self-reported, and the report does not test how much of the result the payments caused.

Sequencing towards commercial capital

Combining these instruments with private capital is the logic of blended finance: public or philanthropic money takes a catalytic role so that private investment follows. According to Convergence's State of Blended Finance 2026, blended finance reached USD 25.8 billion across 181 transactions in 2025, and private sector commitments rose by 27 per cent to USD 11.2 billion. Each dollar of concessional capital supported USD 5.33 in commercial financing and mobilized USD 2.57 in private investment. These ratios are averages across all deals and do not show whether the investment would have taken place without the concession. Convergence's own analysts have also noted that concessional capital often mobilizes public commercial funding from development banks and development finance institutions rather than private investment.

De-risking works best as a pathway over several years. An enterprise may begin with a grant to test its model, move to concessional or impact-linked finance once it shows results, and then graduate to commercial loans or equity. Graduation criteria agreed at the outset keep the support temporary and make the direction clear to the enterprise and to the next investor. The hand-over also needs planning: the follow-on investor should be identified, and in contact with the enterprise, well before the grant ends.

In my work on grant portfolios and impact-linked finance for small tourism businesses, the technical assistance component often decided whether a first loan was repaid. I would budget for it from the start, and not as an add-on.

Avoiding the pitfalls

Poorly designed grants can crowd out private capital, distort prices or create dependency. An additionality test helps at the design stage: would the investment happen without the grant, and does the grant make the enterprise stronger or only cheaper to finance? Grant-makers also have to accept that some supported enterprises will fail. My view is that a portfolio without any failures is probably not taking the risks that justify a grant in the first place. Publishing terms, results and failures, open by default as the Open Data Charter principles recommend, allows others to learn and keeps public and philanthropic money accountable.

Design principles

  • Target the specific risk that blocks investment, not the general cost of doing business.
  • Use the smallest grant element that unlocks the investment, in line with the commitment of development finance institutions to use "only the minimum concessionality needed".
  • Link grant support to milestones and to a clear exit.
  • Combine finance with business support, so that enterprises can use the capital well.
  • Publish results as open data, so that the evidence base for blended finance grows with every transaction.

What this means in practice

For grant-makers and investors preparing a transaction, these principles translate into a few concrete steps.

  • Name the risk and the next investor. At the design stage, state which risk the grant removes and who is expected to invest next, on what terms. If no follow-on investor can be named, the grant may be covering operating costs rather than investment risk.
  • Compare instruments before choosing a grant. Check whether a guarantee or a first-loss tranche would cover the same risk with less money disbursed.
  • Report mobilization in two parts. Show private and public co-finance separately, and set out the case for additionality next to any leverage ratio.
  • Pay for independent verification. Results-based and impact-linked payments need outcome checks by a party other than the implementer. Roots of Impact reports that one such verification, of an impact-linked deal with a chain of diabetes clinics in Mexico, found a causal but small effect on the share of low-income patients.
  • Agree the exit at signature. Set graduation criteria before the first disbursement, and plan from the start how terms and results, including failures, will be published.

Public and philanthropic grant-makers hold capital that can accept risk without expecting a financial return. I see its best use in taking on the risks that markets cannot yet price, and in withdrawing once they can. Used in this way, grants mobilize private investment for development that would otherwise not take place.
