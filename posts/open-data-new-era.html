Open data in a new era: building evidence for rural tourism

October 2026 · approximately 6 minutes

As a programme coordinator in the United Nations system, I have worked on building repositories of evidence on rural tourism destinations from open sources, and then a platform to visualize them. Most of the effort went into structuring and checking the material. The experience has convinced me that, while human verification remains critical and risks remain, the opportunities to collect, structure, analyse and display human knowledge are entering a new era.

Open data in the UN system and beyond

International organizations were publishing open data long before the current interest in artificial intelligence. UNdata, which grew out of a project of the United Nations Statistics Division begun in 2005 and was publicly launched in early 2008, brings more than 60 million data points from the UN statistical system and other international agencies together in one place. On 20 April 2010, the World Bank opened its statistical databases to all users, some 2,000 indicators, after having "worked intensively to modernize its storehouse of statistics". The International Aid Transparency Initiative (IATI), launched in Accra in September 2008, agreed its first common standard for publishing development spending in February 2011 and grew from 100 publishers in 2013 to 1,500 in 2022. The Humanitarian Data Exchange (HDX) of the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA), launched in July 2014, now holds more than 20,000 datasets from hundreds of partners.

The policy framework grew alongside these platforms. A World That Counts, the November 2014 report of the Secretary-General's Independent Expert Advisory Group on a Data Revolution for Sustainable Development, stated that "data are the lifeblood of decision-making and the raw material for accountability". In 2017, the General Assembly adopted the global indicator framework for the Sustainable Development Goals (resolution 71/313), which now counts 234 unique indicators; the annual SDG report draws on more than 50 international and regional agencies and on data from over 200 countries and territories. The UN Global Platform, endorsed by the UN Statistical Commission in 2018 and developed between 2018 and 2020, offers statistical offices "a cloud-service ecosystem to support international collaboration", and the Secretary-General's Data Strategy of May 2020, designed jointly by 50 UN entities, presents itself as "a comprehensive playbook for data-driven change".

Each of these initiatives took years of work by dedicated teams, who agreed standards, negotiated with data providers, and cleaned, documented and maintained the data. In my experience, much of the routine part of that work, such as finding, extracting, translating and structuring material, can now be done far faster with machine assistance. Opening the 57th session of the UN Statistical Commission in March 2026, the Under-Secretary-General for Economic and Social Affairs, Li Junhua, according to UN DESA, described the opportunities of AI "to improve timeliness, reduce costs, and expand analytical capacity", and in the same address warned of challenges of "transparency, bias, accountability, and a widening readiness gap among Member States". Agreeing standards and checking results still take people and time.

Progress remains uneven. The OECD's 2025 results of the Open, Useful and Re-usable Data (OURdata) Index find that, within digital government, "open data policies show comparatively low maturity levels". The European Commission's Open Data Maturity Report 2025 puts the average maturity of 36 European countries at 81 per cent and finds that progress on impact has slowed. The Digital Public Goods Alliance reports more than 220 verified digital public goods and 50 members by the end of 2025.

Open data on the global AI agenda

The clearest recent signal has come from the United Nations itself. In August 2025, the General Assembly, by resolution 79/325, established an Independent International Scientific Panel on Artificial Intelligence and a Global Dialogue on Artificial Intelligence Governance. The Panel's 40 members serve in their personal capacity and are to provide "evidence-based scientific assessments synthesizing and analysing existing research", which is itself a task of evidence synthesis.

The Global Dialogue met in Geneva on 6 and 7 July 2026, with more than 3,000 participants and delegations from 163 countries. The co-chairs' summary, which is their own account and not a negotiated text, records: "Open-source technologies and open data were highlighted as important enablers of broader participation". For anyone building open data in a specialized field, this places the work among the conditions for broad participation in AI. The World Bank's Digital Progress and Trends Report 2025 makes a related point, noting that "AI model capabilities hinge on the quantity, quality and diversity of the data".

Tourism is moving in the same direction. UN Tourism's report on the implementation of its programme of work for 2024–2025 refers to "monthly updates and annual data and insights in the UN Tourism Dashboards" and to 31 pilots of the Statistical Framework for Measuring the Sustainability of Tourism in countries and subnational destinations. Its Member States approved "Digital Agenda and Artificial Intelligence" as the theme of World Tourism Day 2026.

Building the repositories

Evidence about rural tourism destinations is scattered across published sources, such as village profiles, project and monitoring reports, news coverage and local websites, often in several languages. The first repository I worked on brought together good practices from villages recognized as Best Tourism Villages by UN Tourism (BTVs). Each practice is linked to its sources, and every quotation is re-found word for word in the saved source before the practice is accepted. Each is graded by the strength of its evidence and tagged to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) to which it contributes: Proven means an official source reports a positive result for the village, with a figure, attributed to the practice; Promising, a reported result with less evidence; Positive signals, what a village does, with no reported result yet. Related evidence collections followed, built on the same method.

Structure and provenance proved more important than volume: a smaller set of practices, each with a source, a verbatim passage and a grade, is more useful to a planner or a funder than a long list of claims that cannot be traced. Quotations are checked against the saved source, because paraphrases drift over successive drafts and web pages change. Strong findings and grade changes are read a second time by a reader who does not see the first grade, which catches errors and corrects for the habits of the first reader. All entries remain drafts until the team has reviewed them.

Machine assistance speeds up search, translation, extraction and drafting considerably; review by people of every quotation and grade remains the slowest part of the work, and is still under way. In a study within six live systematic reviews, published in the Annals of Internal Medicine in November 2025, extraction by a large language model with human verification was 91.0 per cent accurate, against 89.0 per cent for people alone, and neither approach was free of errors. Cochrane, the Campbell Collaboration, JBI and the Collaboration for Environmental Evidence state in a joint position statement that AI and automation in evidence synthesis "should be used with human oversight", and that those conducting a synthesis remain responsible for it.

Making the evidence visible

The visualization platform, still a working prototype, presents the BTV practices on maps, in portraits of individual destinations and in dashboards. It turned out to be a quality check in its own right. A map shows at once where evidence is dense and where it is missing, and the empty areas often reflect how well a place is documented online more than what happens there. Inconsistent place names, duplicates and misplaced coordinates, easy to overlook in a table, stand out on screen, and several corrections to the repositories started there. Neither the repositories nor the prototype are public at this stage.

Design choices carry responsibility as well. A world map raises questions of boundaries and names, so the platform is designed to follow United Nations cartographic conventions. Colour and default settings shape interpretation too: if a practice with Positive signals looks the same as a Proven one, readers will give the two the same weight.

The limits

The risks are real. According to Columbia University School of Nursing, an audit of 2.5 million biomedical papers, published as a research letter in The Lancet, found 4,046 fabricated citations in 2,810 papers published between January 2023 and February 2026, a rate that rose more than 12-fold over the period. Destinations with a strong online presence in major languages are easier to find, for people and machines alike, and risk being over-represented. Provenance is hard to establish when sources are copied and paraphrased across the web, and when machine output is right most of the time, checking tends to become lighter. Governance is taking shape: the European Union's AI Act became generally applicable on 2 August 2026, with some exceptions, and the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI is to assess the evidence at global level.

What this means in practice

For organizations building evidence repositories or data platforms, this experience suggests a few concrete steps.

  • Record provenance at the point of capture. Store the source link and the exact supporting passage with every entry, so that any claim can be traced and re-checked.
  • Grade the evidence and show the grade. A simple, documented scale tells users how much weight each entry can bear, in every map and dashboard.
  • Plan for a blind second reading. Budget staff time for a second reader who grades findings without seeing the first assessment.
  • Treat machine output as a draft. Have a person check every quotation, citation and grade against the original.
  • Build on what international organizations already provide. Use their open standards, licences and cartographic conventions, and publish methods and design choices with the data.

The open data platforms of international organizations took years to build. Work on a similar scale for a specialized field such as rural tourism is now within reach of a small team. In my view, its results will be trusted to the extent that every entry can be traced to its source and has been checked by a person.
