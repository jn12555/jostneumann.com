Machine intelligence and a new era of tourism research

October 2026 · approximately 5 minutes

Much of my recent work has involved building open data tools and evidence databases, and machine intelligence has changed how I do it. It is changing how evidence and data on tourism are collected, read and used. In my own work, a review of the literature that once took months of desk work can now be prepared in days. The time saved is real, and so is the risk that errors are produced faster than anyone can check them.

From scarce to abundant evidence

Knowledge about tourism, particularly in rural areas, has long been scattered across project reports, evaluations, local news, websites and social media, often in many languages. Collecting it was slow and expensive, so decisions rested on a few well-documented cases, usually from places with strong research institutions. Language models and research agents can now search, read, translate, extract and summarize this material at scale. A small team can build and maintain a structured database of practices, destinations and results that would previously have required a dedicated research unit.

Studies of automated and AI-assisted systematic reviews report that work which usually takes many months can be done in days or weeks, though the output still needs expert checking. In one peer-reviewed case study from 2020, an experienced team using automation tools completed a full systematic review in 61 person-hours over 12 calendar days, against a usual time of about one year. The study comes from medicine and predates current language models.

Collecting data about tourism

Official tourism statistics are built mainly on arrivals, overnight stays and visitor spending, as in the series on international tourist arrivals that the World Bank publishes as open data. They are compiled mainly at national level, and in some countries for large regions. Data for districts and villages, and on who benefits, remain scarce. New sources can fill part of that gap: visitor reviews and other online content, mobility and booking patterns, satellite imagery of land use and environmental change, and sensor data from destinations. Short surveys run by local enterprises and community organizations, collected by phone and analysed with machine support, are another option.

Some of these sources are already part of official statistics. The 2019 UN handbook on mobile phone data for official statistics reports that Statistics Indonesia has used mobile positioning data in its inbound visitor statistics since October 2016, mainly for border posts without immigration checks, and that Estonia's central bank has used such data in travel statistics since 2008. Combined with official statistics, and with quality rules in the spirit of the UN Fundamental Principles of Official Statistics, such as transparency about sources and methods and the confidentiality of individual data, these sources can show how tourism develops in places that conventional surveys do not cover.

Much of the new data is held privately by platforms and operators. In the European Union, large online platforms share aggregated booking data with Eurostat, which publishes them as official statistics: guests spent 951.6 million nights in short-stay accommodation booked through these platforms in 2025, with figures available down to regional level. My view is that agreements of this kind, which make aggregated data available for public-interest research and publish it as open data where possible, would add more to the evidence base for tourism planning and investment decisions than most new tools.

A changing research workflow

The most productive workflows combine machine speed with human judgement. Agents search for sources and extract candidate findings; researchers verify each claim against the original, grade the strength of the evidence and decide what to publish. A second reader who assesses the findings without seeing the first assessment reduces bias. Recording sources, search steps and versions, and publishing the results in line with the FAIR data principles (findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable), makes the work reproducible and open to correction. In building open evidence databases, I have found that once the search is automated, verification takes most of the time and deserves most of the budget.

Risks to take seriously

  • Fabrication. Models can produce convincing but false statements and citations. In a peer-reviewed test from 2023, 55 per cent of the citations produced by GPT-3.5 and 18 per cent of those produced by GPT-4 were fabricated. A 2026 preprint that audited ten commercial language models found fabrication rates between 11.4 and 56.8 per cent, depending on model, field and prompt. Every figure and quotation has to be traced to its source.
  • Visibility bias. Destinations with a strong online presence and content in major languages are over-represented, while others remain invisible, however good their practice.
  • Privacy and consent. Data about people and communities must be collected and published with care, and with their agreement where possible.
  • Extraction. Data drawn from communities should return value to them, and not only to researchers and investors.
  • Environmental cost. Large-scale computation consumes energy, which should be weighed against the value of the result.

Principles for responsible use

  • Verify every figure and quotation against the original source before use.
  • Keep people accountable for conclusions; machine intelligence assists and does not decide. The UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence lists human oversight and determination among its principles and states that "an AI system can never replace ultimate human responsibility and accountability".
  • Publish data and methods openly, under clear licences such as Creative Commons licences, so that others can check and build on the work.
  • Involve communities and local researchers in deciding what is collected and how it is used.
  • Disclose where machine intelligence was used in research and writing.

What this means in practice

For researchers, statistical offices and funders of tourism research, this suggests a few concrete steps.

  • Treat every model output as a lead. Open the original source for each figure and quotation, record where and when it was found, and keep a copy.
  • Budget for checking. Plan more staff time for verification than for search, and add a second reader who grades the evidence without seeing the first assessment.
  • Start from methods already in official use. Mobile positioning and platform booking data are used in official statistics in some countries; the documented methods are a better starting point than new, untested collection.
  • Build confidentiality into data-sharing agreements. Agreements with platforms and operators should settle aggregation, the protection of individual data and the right to publish results before any data change hands.
  • State coverage. Report which languages, sources and periods were searched, so that readers can judge how far visibility bias affects the results.

The tools now available can give destinations, governments and investors a much better picture of what works in tourism for development and why. In my view, whether that picture is trusted will depend on whether its sources can be checked, which is the strongest argument I know for publishing evidence on tourism as open data.
